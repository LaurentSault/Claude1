## 1. How to Use This Appendix

This is the team's fact desk: whether Chopin could be at a given door on a given Tuesday, or whether Lucile could buy a tube of cadmium yellow. Where this file and the canon disagree, **the canon wins** (CANON §0b); corrections the canon needs are logged as **CCRs** in the final section and are not used until the Lead Designer amends the canon.

**Confidence labels** (used in §2, §4 and §6):

| Label | Meaning | How writers may use it |
|---|---|---|
| **Verified** | Attested by letters, memoirs, official records or a standard biography in §10 | Freely, including in Almanach text |
| **Range** | The record gives a span ("August 1833"), not a day | The game picks a day inside the span; never contradict the span |
| **Unverified** | We could not find a primary attestation; a plausible convention is used | Use the game value; never put it in an in-world document as fact |
| **Liberty** | The game knowingly departs from the record | Only as registered in §6, with its in-fiction justification |

**Three rules the research imposes on every doc.**
1. **The record outranks convenience.** A documented first meeting, wedding, death, premiere or Salon is a bar-line (LAW-15). The game stages around it (CANON §16e); it never moves it.
2. **No invented quotation is attributed to a real person outside the game's own dialogue.** Almanach entries, tips and marketing never present game lines as things Chopin or Sand "said"; quote only what §10 can footnote.
3. **Dates are Gregorian, Paris mean time**, weekdays as computed (CANON header). Every 1833–34 weekday the canon gives was rechecked for this appendix and is correct.

## 4. Research Notes: Historical Figures

Dates and ages follow CANON §5a and §7; historical_cast.md owns NPC biographies. This section records each figure's **real situation, spring 1833 to spring 1834**, and the **research flags**.

### 4.1 The six historical party members

**PC-03 Hector Berlioz (29 → 30).** Back from Rome in November 1832, he wins Harriet Smithson with the concert of 9 Dec 1832, nurses her through the broken leg and the debts of 1833, and marries her on Thu 3 Oct 1833, Liszt a witness. He lives by expedients and begins to earn from criticism; *Harold en Italie* (Sun 23 Nov 1834) grows from Paganini's request of January 1834. *Flags:* he did not conduct his own concerts until 1835 (R-29); Narcisse Girard conducted for him in 1833–34. His *Mémoires* are vivid and self-dramatising: use them for voice, the *Correspondance générale* for facts.

**PC-04 Eugène Delacroix (34 → 35).** At 15 quai Voltaire (1829–35). *Liberty Leading the People* (Salon of 1831) was bought by the state and soon taken off view as too inflammatory. In January–July 1832 he travelled in Morocco with the Comte de Mornay's embassy, with a few days in Algiers in late June; from those notebooks comes *Women of Algiers in Their Apartment* (Salon of 1834). In 1833 Thiers gives him the Salon du Roi at the Palais Bourbon, his first great decorative commission. *Flags:* his *Journal* lapses from 1824 to 1847, so in 1833 he "keeps notes" (CANON §16b); his throat trouble is real and chronic; his friendship with Chopin is documented only from about 1838 (R-00) and with Sand from 1834.

**PC-05 Charles-Valentin Alkan (19 → 20).** Born Charles-Valentin Morhange; his father, Alkan Morhange, ran a school for Jewish children in the Marais, and all the siblings took his first name as their surname. A Conservatoire prodigy (premier prix in piano at 10, in 1824; harmony 1827; organ 1834), in 1833 he teaches and has published *Les Omnibus*, Op. 2 (1829) and the *Concerto da camera* No. 1 (1832). *Flags:* his withdrawal from public life comes later (from 1838, and decisively after 1848); his devout Judaism and his Bible studies are to be written with respect (CANON §16e); *Le chemin de fer* is 1844 (LAW-18, BQ-PC05).

**PC-06 Frédéric Chopin (23; 24 on Sat 1 Mar 1834).** In Paris since autumn 1831, debut at Salle Pleyel on Sun 26 Feb 1832; by 1833 he lives by teaching aristocratic pupils at 20 F a lesson. He moves to 5 rue de la Chaussée-d'Antin in June 1833, when the Études Op. 10 (dedicated to Liszt) appear; the E-minor Concerto, Op. 11, follows that year, the *Variations brillantes*, Op. 12, late in 1833, and the Nocturnes, Op. 15, in January 1834. August 1833 at Le Côteau (§2). The G-minor Ballade was begun in 1831 and published in 1836. *Flags:* the Rhineland trip is May 1834 (R-21); Poland's fall (Warsaw, September 1831) is his wound; his tuberculosis is never diagnosed or named on screen (CANON §16e).

**PC-07 Franz Liszt (21 → 22).** In Paris since 1823, with his widowed mother since 1827 (§2). The Paganini concert of 20 Apr 1832 remade his technique; Berlioz has been his friend since 1830. He meets Marie d'Agoult around the turn of 1832–33. In 1833 he transcribes the *Fantastique* for piano (published 1834), works on the *Clochette* fantasy (1832–34) and writes the single piano piece *Harmonies poétiques et religieuses* (1833). *Flags:* a salon performer, not yet the touring phenomenon of 1839–47; his religious fervour is early and real; his minor orders (1865) explain "the Abbé Liszt" (LAW-24).

**PC-08 Louise Farrenc (29).** Born Jeanne-Louise Dumont into a family of sculptors (her brother Auguste Dumont will make the *Génie de la Liberté* atop the July Column), pupil of Reicha, married to Aristide since 1821, mother of Victorine (b. 1826). In 1833 she performs, composes piano works and sketches what becomes the *Air russe varié*, Op. 17 (1835; published 1836; R-58); her Overture No. 1 is 1834. *Flags:* professor only from 1842 (R-48); equal pay follows her Nonet's 1850 premiere; *Le Trésor des pianistes* runs 1861–72; art. 442 really bars her from Théo's *tutelle* (LAW-23.1).

### 4.2 George Sand (NPC-14), the heroine's anchor

Aurore Dudevant (28 → 29) left Nohant for Paris in January 1831 by arrangement with Casimir, earned small sums painting snuffboxes and cigar cases and writing for *Le Figaro*, co-wrote *Rose et Blanche* with Jules Sandeau (1831), then alone *Indiana* (May 1832), *Valentine* (November 1832) and *Lélia* (August 1833). She lives at 19 quai Malaquais with Solange, wears men's clothes to move freely and cheaply, smokes, meets Musset in June 1833, goes with him to Fontainebleau in August and leaves with him for Italy on Thu 12 Dec 1833 (§2). Legal separation from Casimir: 1836. *Flags:* her documented first meetings with Liszt (autumn 1834), Delacroix (1834, the Buloz portrait) and Chopin (late 1836) are bar-lines (R-56, CANON §16e); her October 1833 stay at Nohant is R-17. Lucile, her "younger friend", is fiction: nothing in *Histoire de ma vie* may be quoted as mentioning her.

## 5. Research Notes: Places and Institutions

**Conservatoire (LOC-P03, LOC-P35).** Founded 1795 in the former Hôtel des Menus-Plaisirs, rue Bergère. Its concert hall (1811), famed for its acoustics, housed Habeneck's Société des Concerts (first concert 9 Mar 1828), which taught Paris Beethoven, and the *Fantastique*'s premiere; it was also hired for private concerts (Berlioz, Hiller). Cherubini barred foreigners as a rule and bent it for talent. Women studied piano and voice; none held a senior professorship before Farrenc (1842).

**Salle Pleyel (LOC-P12).** Pleyel & Cie stood at 9 rue Cadet; its salon-sized room staged Chopin's debut (1832) and served until the move to rue Rochechouart in 1839. Pleyel's light single-escapement action was the pianists' favourite; Érard's double escapement (patented 1821) was the rival school. Felt hammers (Pape, 1826) and iron bracing were new: Min-jun's ear for actions (CANON §5f) belongs in this workshop world.

**The Opéra at the Salle Le Peletier (LOC-P27).** Built in 1821 as a "temporary" house after the Salle Montansier was pulled down following the Duc de Berry's assassination at its door (1820); fire destroyed it in 1873. Under Louis Véron (1831–35) it staged *Robert le diable* (1831) and *La Sylphide* (1832), lit by gas since 1822 and famous for its machinery and flying wires: exactly the rafters CH-13 needs. Benefits were common practice (R-29).

**The Louvre (LOC-P13, LOC-E01).** In 1833 a museum of old masters in the Grande Galerie and the Salon Carré, with Egyptian rooms (Musée Charles X, 1827); living artists hung in the Musée du Luxembourg (R-59). Copyists, women included, worked under licence at easels in the galleries (R-09). Each spring the Salon took over the Salon Carré and parts of the Grande Galerie, and screens covered old masters. Watteau's *Pilgrimage to Cythera* (1717) hung here, which matters for REP-07 (§8).

**Faubourg Saint-Germain (LOC-P14, P15, P34).** The old nobility's quarter of *hôtels* between court and garden (rue de Varenne, rue Saint-Dominique, rue de Grenelle). After July 1830 much of the legitimist nobility withdrew from the Orléans court and "sulked" here; embassies rented the grandest houses (the Austrians at the Hôtel de Monaco).

**The catacombs and quarries (LOC-P01, P29).** Centuries of quarrying left galleries under the Left Bank; after the collapses of the 1770s the Inspection générale des carrières (1777) shored and mapped them. From 1785–86 bones from the Cimetière des Innocents and other graveyards went to the Tombe-Issoire quarries by the Barrière d'Enfer, and Héricart de Thury arranged the ossuary (1810–14). Public visits were suspended around 1833 and resumed on a limited basis from about 1850: in March 1833 the galleries hold quarry inspectors, the octroi and wine smugglers dodging the Farmers-General wall, not tourists (R-03).

**The sewers (LOC-P17).** On 1 Jan 1832 Paris had 40.3 km of sewers, built at about 750 m a year since Bruneseau's survey of 1805–12; Parent-Duchâtelet described their filth (1824). Building sped up after the cholera; the great system is Belgrand's, under the Second Empire. The Palais-Royal's cellars and drains are real; LOC-P17's scale is R-14.

**Val-de-Grâce (LOC-P26).** Founded by Anne of Austria after her vow for an heir (Louis XIV, b. 1638); church by François Mansart and Lemercier, dome fresco by Pierre Mignard (1663). A military hospital since 1793–96, treating cholera in 1832, built over consolidated quarries. Delorme's *Vow of Anne of Austria* panel is fictional; its real cousin is Ingres's *Vow of Louis XIII* (1824), which an 1833 Parisian would know.

**The Panthéon (LOC-P28, P30, P38).** Soufflot's Sainte-Geneviève (begun 1764, finished by Rondelet 1790), made the Panthéon in 1791, returned to worship in 1806, secularised again on 26 Aug 1830. David d'Angers carved the pediment from 1831 to 1837 (CH-15's scaffolding). The crypt holds Voltaire (1791) and Rousseau (1794); quarries run beneath the hill, beside Saint-Étienne-du-Mont and the École Polytechnique (there since 1805). Foucault's pendulum is 1851: never mention it.

**Nohant (LOC-R03) and the Berry.** The manor near La Châtre (Indre), inherited by Aurore from her grandmother Marie-Aurore de Saxe (d. 1821); Casimir ran the farms. Sand called the country the Vallée Noire; its wolf-leaders (*meneux de loups*) reappear in her *Légendes rustiques* (1858; BOSS-13). The famous summers of Liszt and d'Agoult (1837), Chopin (1839–46) and Delacroix (1842–43) lie ahead: the game shows the house before its legend.

**The bar-line.** The Société anonyme des artistes peintres, sculpteurs, graveurs opened its first exhibition on Wed 15 Apr 1874 in Nadar's former studio, 35 boulevard des Capucines, with some thirty artists; Louis Leroy's mocking review in *Le Charivari* (25 Apr), "L'Exposition des impressionnistes", named the movement after Monet's harbour. LAW-15 fixes the date; LAW-21 lets the 64-year-old Lucile stand in that room.

### 7.2 Why 1833 can almost see it: Delacroix and Corot as ancestors

- **Delacroix** saw Constable's *Hay Wain* at the Salon of 1824, beside his own *Massacres de Scio*, and by tradition reworked passages after seeing it. He built colour from juxtaposed touches and watched coloured shadows and complementaries, as his later *Journal* records. The Impressionists claimed him: Fantin-Latour painted his *Hommage à Delacroix* (1864) with Manet among the mourners; van Gogh copied his *Pietà* at Saint-Rémy (1889); Signac's *D'Eugène Delacroix au néo-impressionnisme* (1899) traced the whole lineage back to him. His fascination with Lucile's canvas is that historiography run backwards.
- **Corot** painted oil studies out of doors in Italy (1825–28) and at Fontainebleau, in the tradition Valenciennes codified in his *Éléments de perspective pratique* (1800). Pissarro listed himself as "pupil of Corot" in the Salon catalogues of the 1860s, and Berthe Morisot studied with him. GST-03's *SOUVENIR* and his "You paint as I dream" sit exactly on that seam.
- **Science.** At the Gobelins tapestry works, the chemist Michel-Eugène Chevreul was already researching simultaneous colour contrast; his treatise appeared in 1839. A line about "a chemist at the Gobelins who says colours change their neighbours" is period-safe and true.
- **Literature.** Balzac's *Le Chef-d'œuvre inconnu* (1831), set in rue des Grands-Augustins, is about a painter whose masterpiece dissolves into colour that his peers cannot read. Lucile lives in that street. Cézanne later saw himself in its painter, Frenhofer, and Picasso painted *Guernica* (1937) at no. 7.

### 7.4 The ethics frame

Pillar P2 and LAW-20 govern her art. Min-jun gives her an idea ("don't paint outlines"), never a picture to copy. Her canvases are *in the manner of* Monet, Pissarro and van Gogh, and the game names them only in Min-jun's private speech (CANON §16g). The period calls her work ***la manière lumineuse***, never "Impressionism". Three rules follow: (1) no real painting's composition ever appears on her easel (her subjects are 1833 Paris, the Berry and the Panthéon roof); (2) the real painters keep their credit: no epilogue implies that Monet "learned it from her"; (3) characters, not narration, carry the moral weight: Delorme accuses (CH-12), Farrenc objects (BQ-PC08), and Lucile answers, "You asked me. They never asked anyone." LUMIÈRE, her own manner, is the canon's resolution: she ends by painting what no one taught her.

**Hazard: the title *Gaspard de la nuit* exists in 1833.** Ravel's suite takes its title and its three poems from Aloysius Bertrand (1807–1841), who is alive in Paris in 1833, poor and known to the Romantic circles. His manuscript was accepted by a publisher that year and appeared only in 1842, after his death, with help from his friend David d'Angers (NPC-44). A foreigner announcing "*Gaspard de la nuit*" at a salon would therefore be naming a living poet's unpublished book. "Ondine" alone is harmless: the water spirit has been familiar since Fouqué's *Undine* (1811). Recommended handling is in the final section.

**Originals and other repertoire.** REP-27–30 are project-owned. **REP-31 *Arirang*:** many regional Arirangs are old, but the standard (Bonjo) melody most Koreans hum was popularised by Na Woon-gyu's 1926 film, so Min-jun's hum is a "future" tune too; Heat 0 stands because no Parisian could know it. Homer Hulbert's 1896 transcription is the first Western notation of an Arirang. REP-32 (Bach, 1722), REP-33 (Beethoven, published 1799) and REP-34 (Chopin, Op. 9, published 1832–33) are period-safe.

**1830s repertoire (CANON §15e), checked.** Correct as listed, with two refinements: Thalberg's pieces are now named (§2), and Czerny's *School of Velocity*, Op. 299, belongs to the early 1830s, so it is safely available in November 1833.
